# Fair credit pricing and gamification

Status: proposal, 2026-09-29. Synthesized from four research notes: [gamification](evidence/2026-09-29-fair-credit-pricing/gamification.md), [fair pricing](evidence/2026-09-29-fair-credit-pricing/fair-pricing.md), [credit design](evidence/2026-09-29-fair-credit-pricing/credit-design.md), [abuse prevention](evidence/2026-09-29-fair-credit-pricing/abuse-prevention.md). External figures come from search summaries; verify before publishing. Decisions live in [ADR-0006](../adr/adr-0006-the-ticket-is-the-billing-unit.md); this record informs them.

## Make the credit mean one accepted change

Glide should keep credits, but only as a name for one thing: **one credit is one accepted small change, with a fixed, published euro price (€15 list, €12 floor)**. Credits pay only the delivery fee. Tokens, previews and every other meter stay in euros, and every credit amount appears next to its euro value. The rule that makes this ethically explainable is already in the decided model: **model cost passes through at a published 1.25x and is capped before spend, and Glide's profit, the delivery fee, is earned only when the agency accepts the work**. That maps directly onto the best-established fairness research: buyers accept prices justified by the seller's costs and resent gains taken from their dependence. Two decided settings now contradict that story and should be revisited. The **2x credit multiplier for premium models** charges the model premium twice, because tokens already pass through. The rule that **model price changes move the credit price** ties a fee that no longer carries token cost to token prices. Gamification should reward the agency's acceptance quality, never its activity or spend. The strongest mechanisms are a pre-Run readiness check, a private acceptance score with a minimum sample, and published trust tiers that unlock material terms. Streaks, public leaderboards, random bonuses and small bonus credits either distort behavior or crowd out professional motivation. Abuse of pay-on-acceptance is best handled by a layered stack. Tokens are always charged, so rejection is never free. "Accepted" has a precise definition with a symmetric reversal right. Detection runs from exact commit matches up to fuzzy similarity, and only the high-confidence rungs bill after notice. A licence to the code that takes effect only on acceptance backs the rest in contract. **Caveat:** the research relied on search-result summaries, because the environment's proxy blocked almost every direct page fetch. Vendor figures, legal positions and study results below are "reported" and must be checked against the primary source before they appear in any customer-facing material.

## A credit earns its place only as a named outcome with a fixed euro price

The 2025-2026 market has settled one question and left another open. The settled part: developer tools that hid money behind abstract units retired or reworked them after complaints, and moved to credits pegged to a fixed currency amount. Since 1 June 2026 **one GitHub AI Credit is $0.01**, metered on actual tokens ([GitHub Blog](https://github.blog/news-insights/company-news/github-copilot-is-moving-to-usage-based-billing/)). Codex credits are worth about **$0.04** since April 2026 ([OpenAI Codex rate card](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/20001106-codex-rate-card)). Cursor replaced "500 fast requests" with "$20 of usage at API rates" and then had to apologise and refund unexpected charges ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/07/07/cursor-apologizes-for-unclear-pricing-changes-that-upset-users/)). Replit's effort-based pricing drew complaints that the cost was unknown until the agent had finished and that failed tasks were charged ([InfoWorld](https://www.infoworld.com/article/4059876/replit-update-sparks-developers-dissatisfaction-over-pricing.html)). Backlash follows three triggers: less value for the same price, a cost revealed only after the work, and charges for failure. The open question is whether credits help buyers at all. Kyle Poyar calls AI credits "great for vendors" but "a nightmare for customers" who juggle many vendors' units, and "a lifeline but not the AI pricing endgame" ([Growth Unhinged](https://kylepoyar.substack.com/p/ai-credit-pricing)). A pricing critique lists the failure modes: credits expose cost structure, invite exchange-rate games and hide the pain of paying until renewal ([softwarepricing.com](https://softwarepricing.com/blog/credit-based-pricing-ai/)).

Glide's delivery credit escapes most of this because it is an **outcome unit**, not a compute unit. Its closest precedent is Intercom Fin's **$0.99 per resolution**, charged once however many actions Fin takes ([Fin help](https://fin.ai/help/en/articles/13975800-fin-pricing-outcomes)). Fin also shows the founder's unease is justified: an outcome price does not need a credit wrapper at all. A credit layer pays for itself only when it does a job euros cannot. Billing vendors describe that job as unifying several billable things into one balance, prepaying or pooling, or allocating spend flexibly ([Lago](https://getlago.com/blog/credit-based-pricing); [Metronome](https://docs.metronome.com/guides/pricing-packaging/billing-model-guides/prepaid-credits)). Glide passes that test on two counts. Its platform fees include credits (1, 10 and 30), and agencies can hold and allocate credits per client, much as they already sell *strippenkaarten*. If Glide ever moved to purely postpaid billing with no included allowance, it should drop credits and print "S €15 · M €45 · L €120, charged when you accept". Until then, the way to remove the "extra layer" feeling is to make the credit mean one concrete thing and never let it mean anything else.

### The recommended definition

**One Glide credit is the delivery fee for one accepted S-sized change. Its list price is €15, falling to €14, €13 and €12 with volume, and never below €12. An M change costs 3 credits and an L costs 8. Credits pay for nothing except accepted deliveries.** Two reasons support this definition over a pure currency peg such as "1 credit = €1". It names what the buyer gets, and it keeps S/M/L at small whole numbers that match the Fibonacci story points agencies already use. The widening gaps in that scale exist because uncertainty grows with size ([LogRocket](https://blog.logrocket.com/product-management/fibonacci-story-points-guide/)). Because S equals one credit, any whole-credit bundle can be spent down to zero. That matters: EU consumer authorities single out virtual-currency bundles that leave unusable remainders as a practice that forces extra purchases ([European Commission, CPC key principles](https://commission.europa.eu/document/download/8af13e88-6540-436c-b137-9853e7fe866a_en?filename=Key+principles+on+in-game+virtual+currencies.pdf)).

### Display rules

| Rule | What it looks like | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Euros always next to credits, in the same type size | "M · 3 credits (€45)" | Real-money display is the first CPC principle and a named aim of the proposed Digital Fairness Act ([Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/technology-quotient/the-eus-proposed-digital-fairness-act-a-game-developers-guide-to-potential-imp-102ltio)) |
| Invoices are in euros; credits are a descriptive column | "Delivery fee, WI-1042, 3 credits @ €14 = €42.00" | Removes the conversion step at the moment of paying |
| Tokens, previews, CI and storage are never shown in credits | "Model usage €9.13" | Mixing tokens into credits brings back the hidden-markup critique |
| Platform fees show their split | "€249 = 10 credits (€150) + platform €99" | The buyer sees what the subscription buys beyond credits |
| Credits bought at different prices keep their price | "3 credits from October pack (€14)" | Grandfathering is what Copilot's annual-plan customers got and what Cursor's customers lacked ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/reference/copilot-billing/request-based-billing-legacy/copilot-requests)) |
| Reserved credits are visible as reserved | "3 credits reserved for WI-1042, returned if not accepted" | ADR-0006 already returns credits for a Shift that ends without delivery |
| Price and cap shown before the Shift starts | "Estimated model cost €3-6, never more than €10. Delivery fee €45 only if you accept." | Replit's backlash centred on prices visible only afterwards ([usecarly](https://www.usecarly.com/blog/replit-agent-pricing-explained/)) |
| Changes to the euro value are announced ahead and never change the S/M/L counts | Price moves from €15 to €16 for new purchases | Changing what a unit buys was the move behind the "you will get less but pay the same" headlines ([Visual Studio Magazine](https://visualstudiomagazine.com/articles/2026/04/27/devs-sound-off-on-usage-based-copilot-pricing-change-you-will-get-less-but-pay-the-same-price.aspx)) |

### Expiry: purchased credits should outlive the 12-month default

ADR-0006 currently sets **12-month validity with one month of rollover**. That matches the market default, since OpenAI's purchased credits expire a year after purchase ([OpenAI service credit terms](https://openai.com/policies/service-credit-terms/)). It is also the policy that generates the most visible resentment. Recurring community threads complain of paid credits expiring without warning, one reporting **$1,060 lost** ([OpenAI community](https://community.openai.com/t/1-060-in-prepaid-api-credits-expired-without-a-single-notification/1377889)). Accounting standards let vendors plan unredeemed "breakage" as revenue ([Zenskar](https://www.zenskar.com/finance-glossary/credit-breakage)). That is precisely the income a company that promises to profit only on accepted work cannot defend. A search summary also reported that German law generally treats the expiry of prepaid consumer funds as an unfair disadvantage; that claim is unverified. In B2B, Dutch general terms are tested for being "unreasonably onerous" (art. 6:233 BW), and small businesses get some protection through that test ([Maak Law](https://www.maak-law.com/contract-law-netherlands/forbidden-terms-and-conditions-in-netherlands-contracts/)). A forfeiture clause against a five-person agency is the clause most likely to be challenged.

The recommended policy has three classes. **Purchased credits do not expire while the account is active, and unused purchased credits are refunded at their purchase price when the account closes.** That refund also pre-empts the EU Data Act's ban on switching charges from January 2027 ([Alston & Bird](https://www.alston.com/en/insights/publications/2025/09/eu-data-act-switching-requirements-cloud-services)). **Credits included in a platform fee roll over for two months and then lapse**, like Lovable's monthly credits ([Lovable docs](https://docs.lovable.dev/introduction/credits-and-usage)). They are part of a subscription, not a prepayment, and a short rollover keeps a slow month from feeling like a loss. **Promotional and referral credits are marked as such, expire after 90 days and burn first.** Metronome's default of spending zero-cost credits before paid ones follows the same order ([Metronome](https://docs.metronome.com/guides/pricing-packaging/apply-credits-and-commits/prioritization-rules)). The burn order is therefore promotional, then included (oldest first), then purchased (oldest price first). Any lapse is announced 30 and 7 days ahead in the product and by email. Glide should keep credits spendable only on Glide, non-transferable and not cashable except by the closure refund. That keeps them inside the PSD2 limited-network exclusion ([Schoenherr](https://www.schoenherr.eu/content/limited-network-exemption-under-psd2-eba-consults-on-draft-guidelines)). It also fits the earlier tax advice that white-label agencies resell their own service, never Glide credits.

## Pass cost through, earn on acceptance: the explanation that survives scrutiny

Kahneman, Knetsch and Thaler's surveys established the frame that still anchors price-fairness research. Customers are entitled to the terms of a **reference transaction**, firms to a **reference profit**. Raising prices because costs rose is acceptable, and raising them to exploit demand is not ([EconBiz abstract](https://www.econbiz.de/Record/fairness-as-a-constraint-on-profit-seeking-entitlements-in-the-market-kahneman-daniel/10005761609)). An action is judged more harshly when it imposes a loss on the customer than when it withholds a gain ([Chicago Booth copy](https://faculty.chicagobooth.edu/-/media/research/cdr/docs/thaler/fairness-as-a-constraint-on-profit-seeking.pdf)). Xia, Monroe and Cox add that fairness covers the **process** as well as the outcome, and that trust and comparison with other customers shape the judgment ([Journal of Marketing](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmkg.68.4.1.42733)). Glide's two-part price fits each clause. The provider's list price is the reference transaction. The disclosed markup and the delivery fee are the reference profit. The per-ticket cap turns a potential loss into a known ceiling. Published per-Run token counts make the process auditable. The honest one-line explanation is: **"You pay what the model costs, plus 25%, never more than the cap. You pay our delivery fee only when you accept the change."**

The 25% markup is the part that will be benchmarked, and Glide should justify it in writing. OpenRouter passes provider rates through with no token markup and takes a **5.5% fee** on credit purchases ([TrueFoundry](https://www.truefoundry.com/blog/openrouter-pricing)). Amp reportedly bills individuals and teams at provider rates with no markup ([G2](https://www.g2.com/products/sourcegraph-sourcegraph/pricing)) but adds **50% for enterprise**, where the premium buys something named ([aitoolsatlas](https://aitoolsatlas.ai/tools/amp-code)). A 25% markup therefore needs a published list of what it pays for: EU-region routing, payment and metering fees, Ploeg's orchestration and sandbox compute, and retries. It should not be presented as a pure cost pass-through. Buffer's "where your money goes" breakdown per subscription dollar is a reusable format ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/transparent-pricing-dashboard/)). Everlane is the warning attached to it: cost transparency drew criticism once it looked selective and unverifiable ([The Ethos](https://the-ethos.co/everlanes-radical-transparency-shein-bargain-buyout/)). Glide should publish its split only once it can compute it from metered data.

Charging tokens for failed attempts is defensible, with one exception to write down. Outcome-priced support products charge nothing for failures ([Intercom](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/7837512-how-fin-pricing-works)). Usage tools where the customer drives the work pass through all tokens, and Devin reportedly does not refund failed sessions ([fast.io](https://fast.io/resources/devin-acu-guide/)). Commentary on agent pricing argues that "charging for failed work is equivalent to charging for harm" ([Pricing Innovation](https://pricinginnovation.substack.com/p/failure-patterns-in-ai-agent-pricing)). Glide sits between these positions: the customer covers the real third-party cost of work it requested, and Glide's margin-bearing fee depends on success. The weak point is that Glide controls prompts, retries and stopping rules, so some failures are Glide's own. Ploeg already separates infrastructure failures from agent failures (Ploeg ADR-0021). **Runs that end in a Glide-side failure (crash, timeout, sandbox or routing error) should be free**, and the receipt should say so. The cap bounds the rest.

The consequence of the two-part price is larger than it looks. The earlier pricing work derived the €12 floor, the 2x premium multiplier and the rule that model price changes move the credit price for a world in which Glide absorbed failed-attempt tokens. Under the decided model the customer pays those tokens. The delivery fee is now almost pure margin, and all three rules have lost their original justification. The 2x multiplier in particular charges a premium model's cost twice: once in tokens and again in credits. That is hard to explain to any buyer who reads the receipt.

Each line answers a question a sceptical buyer would ask. What did the model cost? What did Glide add? How close did the ticket get to its limit? What triggered the fee? How can it be undone? For the agency's own reporting, the monthly view should also show **model cost per accepted ticket including failed attempts**, for example "your S tickets cost €5.10 in model usage per accepted change this month". This is the number an agency needs to set its client prices, and hiding it would be the "two meters" problem in a new form.

## Reward accepted quality, never activity or spend

The evidence on gamification is weaker and more conditional than its marketing suggests. A meta-analysis found **small effects on motivation (g = 0.36) and behavior (g = 0.25)**, and only the cognitive effect held up in the most rigorous studies ([Sailer & Homner, ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1245270)). A review of 24 empirical studies found effects depended heavily on context and users ([Hamari et al., HICSS 2014](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1109/HICSS.2014.377)). What gamification does reliably is **steer**: Stack Overflow badges measurably shifted which activities users pursued ([Anderson et al.](https://cs.stanford.edu/people/ashton/pubs/www-badges.pdf)). When GitHub removed contribution streaks in 2016, weekend activity and single-contribution days fell, which shows the streak had been producing low-value activity ([Moldon et al., ICSE 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.02371)). Small payments can also do worse than none. In "Pay Enough or Don't Pay at All", token incentives reduced performance ([Gneezy & Rustichini](https://rady.ucsd.edu/_files/faculty-research/uri-gneezy/pay-enough.pdf)). For Glide's buyers, agency owners and senior developers, the implication is precise. Whatever Glide counts, agencies will produce more of, so Glide should count only what the agency itself wants: accepted changes with little rework. Rewards should change terms materially or give useful feedback, not hand out trinkets.

The B2B programs that last share that shape. HubSpot's partner tiers require **75-80% gross revenue retention** at the top and "good standing" with no escalations, so volume alone cannot buy status ([HubSpot](https://www.hubspot.com/partners/how-tiers-work)). Shopify recalculates tiers quarterly against published thresholds ([Shopify tiering guide](https://cdn.shopify.com/static/partner-resources/shopify-partner-program-guide-service-track.pdf)). HackerOne's **Signal** averages reputation per report, penalises invalid reports lightly (Not Applicable −10), leaves honest structural outcomes such as duplicates neutral, and is computed only after more than three closed reports ([HackerOne docs](https://docs.hackerone.com/en/articles/8369891-signal-impact)).

### Ranked mechanisms worth building (proposed)

| Rank | Mechanism | How it works | Why it ranks here |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Readiness check before the Run** | The refinement Run scores a Work Item for acceptance criteria, reproduction steps and scoped files, shows what would raise its chance of acceptance, and flags when it is not agent-ready | Rewards quality with the thing the agency wants most, a higher chance of paying only for accepted work. Nothing to game, and it serves delivery rate, the metric that governs both parties' economics |
| 2 | **Private acceptance score with a benchmark** | Rolling 90-day share of closed Work Items accepted, shown to the agency beside the platform median. Glide-fault failures are excluded, and nothing is shown before 10 closed Work Items | Borrowed from HackerOne Signal. It doubles as an abuse signal (below) and as the proof an agency can show its clients |
| 3 | **Published trust tiers recalculated quarterly** | Thresholds on accepted deliveries *and* acceptance score unlock material terms: more concurrent Shifts, postpaid invoicing, higher default metered cap, a longer included-credit rollover, and optionally a lower token markup | Quality-gated, rule-based and explainable, like Shopify's tiers. No invite-only or discretionary tier |
| 4 | **Refinement credit-back** | Refinement tokens (beyond the free allowance) are credited against the delivery fee if the same Work Item is accepted within 30 days | Agencies credit paid discovery against the build within 2-6 weeks for the same reason ([Sakas & Company](https://sakasandcompany.com/start-using-paid-discovery/)). Removes the "paying twice" objection and rewards conversion rather than volume |
| 5 | **Honest value ledger** | Monthly: accepted changes, model cost per accepted change, euros not charged for rejected work and Glide faults, and the agency's own entered hourly equivalent | Makes the value visible without inventing it. Hourly equivalents come from the agency, not from Glide |
| 6 | **Client-facing delivery record (opt-in)** | The agency may show its end clients its acceptance score and accepted changes in the portal | Turns quality into a sales asset for the agency. Opt-in because it discloses performance |
| 7 | **Referral credits in euros** | A fixed euro value, clearly labelled promotional, credited to the referrer after the referee's first accepted delivery | Common and harmless when shown in euros and triggered by value. DigitalOcean's version rewards the referee's spend and expires in 60 days ([DigitalOcean](https://www.digitalocean.com/referral-program)). Glide should do neither |
| 8 | **Milestone recognition** | Private markers such as "100 accepted changes" or "90% acceptance over a quarter", with no rewards attached | Low effect, low risk. Only worth building once 1-7 exist |

### Patterns to avoid

| Pattern | Why to avoid it |
|---|---|
| Streaks of any kind (daily usage, consecutive accepted tickets) | Streaks produce low-value activity and push people who break one toward churning ([Moldon et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.02371); [The Decision Lab](https://thedecisionlab.com/insights/consumer-insights/streak-creep-the-perils-of-too-much-gamification)) |
| Public leaderboards across agencies | Discloses one customer's performance to its competitors and rewards volume |
| Random, "mystery" or loot-box bonus credits | Variable rewards are a named concern in the Digital Fairness Act work ([Goodwin](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/11/alerts-practices-antc-from-dark-patterns-to-fair-play)) |
| Small bonus credits for good behavior ("0.1 credit for a well-written ticket") | Small payments can crowd out the motivation of professionals ([Gneezy & Rustichini](https://rady.ucsd.edu/_files/faculty-research/uri-gneezy/pay-enough.pdf)) |
| Status or badges based on spend or number of Work Items | Rewards Glide's revenue instead of the agency's outcome, and invites splitting tickets |
| Countdown and scarcity pressure on credits ("5 credits expire in 2 days, top up now") | Loss-aversion pressure on a paid balance. Expiry notices should inform, never sell |
| Nagging top-up prompts and confirmshaming ("Are you sure you want to stop saving?") | The EU fitness check found **48%** of consumers faced repeated requests and **42%** confirmshaming ([William Fry](https://www.williamfry.com/knowledge/world-consumer-rights-day-part-1-digital-fairness-act-the-road-to-fitness-for-online-consumers/)) |
| Prices shown only in credits, or packs that leave unusable remainders | Contrary to the CPC principles on virtual currency ([European Commission](https://commission.europa.eu/news-and-media/news/european-commission-hosts-stakeholders-talks-application-cpc-networks-key-principles-games-virtual-2025-06-03_en)) |
| Costs revealed after the work (drip pricing) | **32%** of consumers in the fitness check saw prices rise during purchase ([William Fry](https://www.williamfry.com/knowledge/world-consumer-rights-day-part-1-digital-fairness-act-the-road-to-fitness-for-online-consumers/)) |
| Discretionary, invite-only tiers | Unexplainable. Use published thresholds |
| Silent price-per-size changes (L from 8 to 10 credits) | "Less for the same price" is the classic trigger for backlash |

Most of these rules come from consumer law, and Glide sells B2B, so they are not strictly binding. Glide should adopt them anyway. The Dutch regulator ACM's guidelines for online persuasion ask companies to test whether users would make the same choice without the influence technique ([ACM](https://www.acm.nl/en/publications/guidelines-protection-online-consumer)). Freelancers and one-person agencies blur the line between business and consumer. The Digital Fairness Act proposal was still expected "before the end of 2026" in the latest summaries seen, with conflicting reports about whether a draft had appeared ([privacylaws.com](https://www.privacylaws.com/news/eu-proposal-on-digital-fairness-act-expected-by-the-end-of-2026/)). Applying consumer-grade rules to everyone costs Glide little and removes the ambiguity. It also turns "ethically explainable" into a claim a third party can check.

## Six layers make taking the code without paying expensive

Glide's pay-on-acceptance model reverses the usual escrow. On Upwork the client funds a milestone before work starts, and silence releases payment after **14 days** ([Upwork](https://support.upwork.com/hc/en-us/articles/211063718-How-payments-for-milestones-and-fixed-price-contracts-work)). On Fiverr the buyer pays up front and a digital delivery auto-completes after **3 days** ([Fiverr](https://help.fiverr.com/hc/en-us/articles/37332473202065-The-complete-guide-to-your-Fiverr-order-Statuses-and-process)). At Glide the customer holds the value, because the code sits in its repository, while Glide holds only a claim. That is the risk profile of Fiverr's "cancel after delivery" complaints. The workable precedent is Intercom Fin. There the provider's own instrumentation defines the outcome, silence counts as success, and a published reversal rule applies when the customer returns: the resolution "will be deducted and not charged" ([Intercom](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/7837512-how-fin-pricing-works)). No single defense survives every evasion, so the stack should grow more expensive to evade at each layer. Only evidence at the top rungs should ever produce a bill. The thresholds below are **proposed starting values** to be calibrated on pilot data. None comes from a source.

| Layer | Defense | Thresholds (proposed) | Fairness guard |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Floor | **Tokens are always charged** (decided) | Every attempt, capped per ticket; Glide-fault Runs free | Rejecting work is never free, but a rejection costs at most the cap |
| 2. Definition | **Precise acceptance and reversal rules** | The 10-working-day timer starts when the PR is marked ready with required checks green. "Changes requested" pauses it. Reminders on days 3 and 7 (decided). Closing requires a reason code. Merge by any route counts as acceptance. Reversal within 14 days for an in-scope defect (decided) | Symmetric: silence counts as yes, and a documented defect counts as a way back. A PR closed with a reason before day 10 is a rejection, never a charge |
| 3. Detection | **Matching-commit ladder** on protected branches, run for **30 days** after a PR closes unaccepted | Rungs: (a) any PR commit SHA reachable from the base branch; (b) the forge reports merged, or `merge_commit_sha` exists; (c) `git patch-id --stable` of a PR commit or the whole diff matches a base-branch commit; (d) at least 60% of the PR's substantive added lines appear in later base-branch commits; (e) winnowing fingerprints over normalised tokens. Rungs (d) and (e) are skipped for PRs under 20 substantive added lines, and lockfiles, generated and formatting-only hunks are excluded throughout | Rungs (a)-(c) notify first and bill only after a 10-working-day dispute window. Rungs (d)-(e) open a review and never bill automatically. A partial match can be billed pro rata only with the agency's agreement |
| 4. Visibility | **Acceptance score shown to the agency** (decided), used as a review trigger | Review when the trailing-90-day acceptance rate falls below half the platform median with at least 20 closed Work Items, when 2 or more rung (a)-(c) matches occur in 90 days, or when reversals exceed 10% of deliveries in 90 days | A flag opens a conversation, not a penalty. Structured rejection reasons also improve refinement |
| 5. Trust tiers | **Graduated terms** tied to a verified billing entity (KvK or VAT number) | New accounts pay the platform fee up front, run up to 3 concurrent Shifts and use prepaid credits. After 10 accepted deliveries, postpaid and higher concurrency. Two confirmed copy events demote the account to prepaid-only for a quarter | Standard B2B onboarding. Demotion is published and rule-based, never discretionary |
| 6. Contract | **Licence on acceptance** | The customer's licence to delivered code takes effect on acceptance. Acceptance includes incorporating substantially the same code into any branch or repository the customer controls, as defined by the ladder. The clause says "the delivery fee becomes due", never "a penalty applies". The customer attests on rejection that the code will not be used | The only defense against moving the code to a repository Glide cannot see |

Each layer rests on a specific finding. `git patch-id` is "a sum of SHA-1 of the file diffs … with line numbers ignored", and `--stable` makes it independent of file order ([git documentation](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/git/git/master/Documentation/git-patch-id.adoc)). That makes it the right tool for cherry-picks and rebases. It cannot follow squashes, because SHAs change and a rebase merge leaves no link back to the PR ([GitHub community](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/6414)). Winnowing, the algorithm behind the MOSS plagiarism detector, guarantees detection of any shared run of at least K+W−1 tokens ([Schleimer et al., SIGMOD 2003](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/872757.872770)). It also fixes the principled minimum size below which a match is likely coincidence. Watermarking generated code does not belong in the stack at all. After minor semantics-preserving edits, true-positive rates fell to **0.22 and 0.11** for two schemes ([arXiv 2403.17983](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.17983v3)). That is too weak to support a bill. Signing Glide's own commits and attaching a per-Run attestation remains useful in disputes, because it proves what Glide produced and when. It proves nothing about a copy.

The contract layer carries a real legal uncertainty. Under Dutch law copyright stays with the maker unless a deed transfers it ([Auteurswet art. 2](https://www.boek9.nl/wetteksten/auteurswet/artikel-2)), and licences conditional on payment are common in Dutch IT practice ([MAAK advocaten](https://www.maakadvocaten.nl/intellectueel-eigendomsrecht/licentie-op-content/)). But purely AI-generated code may attract no copyright at all. The fee obligation should therefore rest on contract, "use triggers the fee", rather than on an infringement claim. Counsel should also check the case of public open-source repositories, where a customer could argue that any contribution is licensed openly.

Two further vectors need explicit rules. **Mis-sizing is mostly self-limiting**: an L declared as S hits the €4 cap and stops, and the cap offers a split. Glide should therefore never raise a delivery fee after the fact, even when the diff turns out larger than the size. It should flag the mismatch for the next refinement instead. Retroactive upcharges are the Replit pattern. **Splitting tickets gains nothing on fees**, because 1/3/8 is roughly linear: three S tickets cost the same as one M, and three M tickets cost more than one L. **Bring-your-own-key accounts are the real gap.** When an agency pays its model provider directly, "tokens are always charged" no longer costs it anything at Glide, and rejection becomes free for the customer, though not for Glide's infrastructure. BYOK accounts need either a small per-attempt orchestration fee or stricter review thresholds in layer 4.

## Ten decisions remain with the founder

| # | Decision | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Keep the word "credit", or rename it in the product ("delivery", "strip") | Keep "credit" in contracts. In the interface, always subtitle it "1 credit = 1 accepted S change = €15" |
| 2 | Premium models at 2x credits | Drop it. Tokens already carry the premium, so 2x charges it twice |
| 3 | Model price changes move the credit price | Decouple. Model prices reach customers through tokens; the credit price should move only for Glide's own cost or value reasons, with notice |
| 4 | Basis for the €12 floor | Re-derive it from platform cost (infrastructure, support, maintainers) rather than worst-case token cost, which customers now pay |
| 5 | Credit expiry: keep 12 months, or adopt "no expiry while active, refund on exit" | Adopt no expiry plus refund. Give up breakage revenue deliberately and say so |
| 6 | Markup on failed attempts | Keep 1.25x on agent failures and charge nothing for Glide-fault Runs. The stricter alternative, no markup on any failed attempt, makes "we never profit from failure" literally true at a small revenue cost |
| 7 | What the 25% markup covers | Publish the list now, and a Buffer-style split once metered data exists |
| 8 | Detection window, auto-bill rungs and whether repository read access after closure is a condition of service | 30 days; only rungs (a)-(c) bill after notice; read access required for hosted plans |
| 9 | Whether trust tiers may lower the token markup | Yes, but only as a published quarterly rule, so good quality buys a better price, not a badge |
| 10 | BYOK abuse gap | Add a small per-attempt orchestration fee for BYOK, or apply stricter layer-4 thresholds |

Several items need specialist sign-off before launch. Counsel must review the licence-on-acceptance clause and the copyright status of AI-generated code, and check whether expiry or forfeiture clauses hold up against small Dutch agencies. A tax adviser must confirm the voucher or advance-payment treatment of credits. Every vendor figure in this report needs checking against its primary page, because the research saw only search summaries: Copilot's and Codex's credit values, Fin's resolution rules, OpenRouter's and Amp's markups, and the CPC and DFA texts. No source gave a measured base rate for "close the PR and copy the diff" abuse, or survey data on what markup B2B buyers consider fair. The design partners should produce both numbers.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the founder's discomfort. The decision to charge tokens on every attempt made the credit simpler than it was designed to be. Credits no longer carry model risk, so they can mean one plain thing, an accepted small change at a fixed euro price. The rules built for the old model, the premium multiplier, the cost-derived floor and credit prices tracking model prices, are now the main source of complexity and the hardest parts to explain. Removing them does more for explainability than any display rule.

The same logic governs gamification and abuse. The only behavior Glide should encourage is the one that also defends it: well-refined tickets that get accepted. A readiness check that raises acceptance, an acceptance score that doubles as an abuse signal, and trust tiers that reward both sit on a single metric. Glide will need that metric regardless, because the delivery rate decides whether the agency's fixed-price promise works at all. Pay-on-acceptance is fair only if acceptance is measured honestly in both directions: Glide finds the code that landed without payment, and it refunds changes that were accepted and then reverted for a defect.
